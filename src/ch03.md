Three types allow motion in two independent directions. A triangle provides a complete picture of their proportions, including mixtures on its edges and pure states at its vertices. Integer population counts supply a natural lattice inside the triangle. These two representations connect geometry with combinatorics.

## Reading a probability triangle

Write $p=(p_A,p_B,p_C)$ with nonnegative entries adding to one. Place the pure states at $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, and $C=(1/2,\sqrt3/2)$. Represent $p$ by the weighted point
$$X=p_AA+p_BB+p_CC=\left(p_B+\frac{p_C}{2},\frac{\sqrt3}{2}p_C\right).$$
These are **barycentric coordinates**: the coefficients tell us how much of each vertex contributes. The midpoint of edge $AB$ is $(1/2,1/2,0)$ in probability coordinates. The center is $(1/3,1/3,1/3)$. A point on edge $AB$ has $p_C=0$.

To recover probabilities from a plotted point $(u,v)$, solve the two coordinate equations:
$$p_C=\frac{2v}{\sqrt3},\qquad p_B=u-\frac{v}{\sqrt3},\qquad p_A=1-u-\frac{v}{\sqrt3}.$$
The point is inside the triangle precisely when these three values are nonnegative.

::: {.example title="Locating a state"}
The distribution $(1/2,1/3,1/6)$ is plotted at $(5/12,\sqrt3/12)$. Its height is one sixth of the triangle's altitude, reflecting its $C$ proportion. Its position is closer to vertex $A$ than to either of the other vertices, consistent with $A$ being the most common type.
:::

## Tokens produce a triangular lattice

Suppose the total count is a fixed integer $N$. A count triple $(a,b,c)$ with $a+b+c=N$ gives the probability vector $(a/N,b/N,c/N)$. The possible states form a lattice. Moving one token from $A$ to $B$ changes the counts by $(-1,1,0)$ and the probabilities by $(-1/N,1/N,0)$.

There are
$$\frac{(N+1)(N+2)}2=\binom{N+2}{2}$$
possible triples. One proof fixes $c$: for $c=0,1,\ldots,N$, there are $N-c+1$ choices for $a$, with $b$ then determined. Adding gives $(N+1)+N+\cdots+1$. A second proof encodes the triple as $N$ stars separated into three groups by two bars; choosing the two bar positions among $N+2$ positions determines the triple.

::: {.example title="A lattice of fifteen states"}
With $N=4$, the lattice has $\binom62=15$ states. The count triple $(2,1,1)$ is adjacent to six states obtained by moving a token between two distinct types. At $(4,0,0)$, only two moves are possible because an empty type cannot donate a token.
:::

A normalized growth update generally does not land on this lattice. It defines a deterministic map on all probability vectors. Sampling $N$ new individuals from the updated proportions produces a random lattice state instead. These are two distinct models with different sources of variation.

## Cyclic interactions

For three strategies, a score table is a $3$ by $3$ matrix. The average score of type $i$ is $f_i=\sum_jM_{ij}p_j$. Consider
$$M_s=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-s\\-s&0&1\\1&-s&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad s>0.$$
Type $A$ gains against $B$, type $B$ gains against $C$, and type $C$ gains against $A$. Each suffers a loss of magnitude $s$ against the remaining opponent. At the center, all three scores equal $(1-s)/3$, so the center is fixed for both normalized multiplier rules.

For exponential multipliers, a step is
$$p_i^+=\frac{p_i e^{\eta f_i}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_jp_j e^{\eta f_j}.$$
The current scores must be recomputed before the next step. Using the same scores repeatedly instead would describe fixed external advantages, rather than this interaction game.

::: {.example title="Computing a cyclic step"}
Let $s=1$ and $p=(1/2,1/3,1/6)$. The scores are $(1/6,-1/3,1/6)$. With $\eta=1$, cancelling the common factor $e^{1/6}$ leaves unnormalized entries $(1/2,e^{-1/2}/3,1/6)$. After normalization the result is approximately $(0.5755,0.2327,0.1918)$. The two equally scoring types retain their ratio $3:1$ during this step.
:::

## A finite inequality detects outward drift

The **weighted arithmetic-geometric mean inequality** states that, for positive $a_i$ and nonnegative weights $p_i$ adding to one,
$$\sum_i p_i a_i\ \geq\ \prod_i a_i^{p_i}.$$
For rational weights, it follows from the ordinary arithmetic-geometric mean inequality by repeating each entry the appropriate number of times. The real-weight version follows by approximation. Equality holds when all entries carrying positive weight are equal. We will use this finite inequality several times.

For the balanced cyclic table $s=1$, direct cancellation gives
$$\sum_i f_i=0,\qquad \sum_i p_if_i=0.$$
Let $P(p)=p_Ap_Bp_C$. Under exponential normalization,
$$P(p^+)=\frac{P(p)e^{\eta(f_A+f_B+f_C)}}{Z^3}=\frac{P(p)}{Z^3}.$$
Applying weighted arithmetic-geometric mean to $a_i=e^{\eta f_i}$ gives $Z\geq e^{\eta\sum_i p_if_i}=1$. For an interior state other than the center, the scores are not all equal, so $Z>1$ and $P$ strictly decreases.

The product is largest at the center: the ordinary arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives $p_Ap_Bp_C\leq(1/3)^3$. Decreasing product therefore records movement through product contours toward the boundary. It does not imply that every individual coordinate moves monotonically, nor does it by itself identify a limit point. A trajectory may continue circulating while this diagnostic decreases.

::: {.proposition title="The balanced finite rule does not conserve the product"}
For every positive intensity, an exponential update of the balanced cyclic game strictly decreases $p_Ap_Bp_C$ at every interior state other than the center.
:::

There is a similar conclusion for linear multipliers whenever they are positive. Since the weighted mean score is zero, their normalizing denominator is $1$. Their unweighted sum is $3$, so their product is at most $1$. Multiplying the three updated coordinates shows $P(p^+)\leq P(p)$, again strictly away from the center. These exact calculations explain why changing the size of a finite step can affect a cyclic picture.

## Comparing a different recurrence

The permutation rule
$$T(p_A,p_B,p_C)=(p_C,p_A,p_B)$$
also moves states cyclically. It satisfies $T^3(p)=p$ and preserves the coordinate product exactly. Its trajectories contain at most three states. This recurrence is useful as a simple example of exact periodicity and as a check on a plotting program. It is a different update from score-based selection, with a different algebraic structure.

## Exercises

::: {.exercises}
1. Plot $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1/2,1/2)$, and $(1/4,1/4,1/2)$ using barycentric coordinates.
2. Recover the probabilities of the point $(u,v)=(1/2,\sqrt3/6)$.
3. List all count triples with $N=2$. Verify the formula for their number.
4. How many lattice states are there for $N=10$? How many have all three counts positive?
5. List the neighbors of $(2,1,1)$ under one-token moves.
6. Compute the scores of the balanced cyclic table at $(1/5,1/2,3/10)$.
7. Verify algebraically that $\sum_i p_if_i=0$ for the balanced table.
8. Derive the product update formula for exponential multipliers for any three scores, without assuming balance.
9. Explain why the product test says nothing new on an edge of the triangle. Determine whether the update can leave that edge.
10. Show that the center is the unique interior state where all balanced cyclic scores agree.
11. For the permutation rule, determine all fixed states and all possible periods.
12. Compare deterministic normalization with sampling a fixed number of individuals after normalization. Which can lose a type in a single step when every initial proportion is positive?
:::

## Selected solutions

**2.** The recovered probabilities are $(1/3,1/3,1/3)$, the center.

**4.** There are $\binom{12}{2}=66$ states. Subtract one token from each positive count; the remaining three nonnegative counts sum to $7$, giving $\binom92=36$ strictly positive states.

**5.** The neighbors are $(1,2,1)$, $(1,1,2)$, $(3,0,1)$, $(2,0,2)$, $(3,1,0)$, and $(2,2,0)$.

**6.** The scores are $(1/5,1/10,-3/10)$.

**8.** Multiplying the three update equations gives $P(p^+)=P(p)e^{\eta\sum_i f_i}/Z^3$. Balance is needed only to simplify the exponential factor to one.

**11.** A fixed state has three equal coordinates and is therefore the center. Every other state has period three, since applying the permutation three times is the identity and a period dividing three is either one or three.
