Probability describes both a population's composition and the uncertainty of an observation. If a population has proportions $p_A,p_B,p_C$ and one individual is chosen uniformly, these proportions are the probabilities of the observed type. Repeating the observation produces a sample. Its composition fluctuates, even when the underlying population stays fixed.

Counting tells us which sample compositions are possible and how many ordered samples produce each one. It supplies exact formulas for uncertainty at a finite sample size.

## Outcomes, events, and trees

An **outcome** is a single possible result. An **event** is a collection of outcomes. For a finite probability model, the probability of an event is the sum of the probabilities of its outcomes. The total probability of all outcomes is one.

Two observations are **independent** when the probability of a specified pair is the product of the individual probabilities. More generally, the probability of an ordered independent sample $(i_1,\ldots,i_N)$ is $p_{i_1}\cdots p_{i_N}$. A tree represents this multiplication: each level is another observation, and the probability of a leaf is the product of the branch probabilities along its path.

::: {.example title="Sampling with and without replacement"}
A box contains two red tokens and one blue token. Two draws with replacement give $P(RB)=(2/3)(1/3)=2/9$. Without replacement, the second draw depends on the first, and $P(RB)=(2/3)(1/2)=1/3$. The independent-sampling formulas below apply to the first procedure. A finite-population procedure without replacement requires its own probabilities.
:::

## Binomial counting

Suppose each observation is a success with probability $p$ and a failure with probability $1-p$. An ordered sample with $k$ successes has probability $p^k(1-p)^{N-k}$. The successes can occupy $\binom Nk$ different sets of positions, where
$$\binom Nk=\frac{N!}{k!(N-k)!},\qquad N!=1\cdot2\cdots N,\quad 0!=1.$$
Therefore the success count $K$ has the **binomial distribution**
$$P(K=k)=\binom Nk p^k(1-p)^{N-k},\qquad k=0,\ldots,N.$$
The binomial coefficient counts positions, while the powers account for their probabilities. Keeping these two roles separate helps prevent common mistakes.

::: {.example title="Exactly three successes"}
With $N=5$ and $p=2/3$, the probability of exactly three successes is
$$\binom53(2/3)^3(1/3)^2=80/243.$$
The probability of one particular ordered sample, such as $SSFSF$, is only $8/243$. There are ten orders with the same count.
:::

Adding the binomial probabilities gives one. This follows either because the counts partition all possible samples or by expanding $(p+(1-p))^N$ and grouping terms by the number of factors $p$.

## Multinomial counts and empirical distributions

For $k$ categories, let $n_i$ be the observed count of category $i$, so $\sum_i n_i=N$. The number of ordered samples with these counts is
$$\Omega(n_1,\ldots,n_k)=\frac{N!}{n_1!\cdots n_k!}.$$
One derivation chooses the positions of category one, then category two among the remaining positions, and continues. The resulting binomial coefficients telescope to the displayed ratio.

The probability of this count vector is
$$P(n_1,\ldots,n_k)=\Omega(n_1,\ldots,n_k)\prod_i p_i^{n_i}.$$
The **empirical distribution** is $\widehat p_i=n_i/N$. It belongs to the token lattice of Chapter 3. Several ordered samples can have the same empirical distribution, and $\Omega$ measures this multiplicity. If $n_i=0$, the factor $p_i^{n_i}$ is taken as one, including when $p_i=0$; no observation of that category is being required.

::: {.example title="A count vector on the triangle"}
For $N=4$ and counts $(2,1,1)$, the multiplicity is $4!/(2!1!1!)=12$. Under $p=(1/2,1/3,1/6)$, each compatible ordered sample has probability $(1/2)^2(1/3)(1/6)=1/72$. The count vector therefore has probability $12/72=1/6$.
:::

## Expectation and spread from finite sums

A **random variable** assigns a numerical value to each outcome. If it takes values $x_i$ with probabilities $p_i$, its **expectation** is
$$\mathbb E[X]=\sum_i p_ix_i.$$
This weighted average describes the center of its distribution. Its **variance** is the average squared distance from that center:
$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_i p_i(x_i-\mathbb E[X])^2
=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb E[X])^2.$$
The last equality follows by expanding the square and using $\sum_i p_i=1$. Variance is nonnegative. Its square root, the **standard deviation**, has the same units as the original readings.

::: {.example title="A finite measurement table"}
Suppose a device reads $0,1,3$ with probabilities $1/2,1/3,1/6$. Its mean is $0+1/3+3/6=5/6$. Its second moment is $0+1/3+9/6=11/6$, so its variance is $11/6-25/36=41/36$. The mean need not be one of the possible readings.
:::

For two random variables, finite-sum expansion gives $\mathbb E[X+Y]=\mathbb E[X]+\mathbb E[Y]$, whether or not they are independent. For independent variables, multiplication of probabilities also gives $\mathbb E[XY]=\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]$. Expanding the square then shows that independent variances add.

Apply these facts to indicator variables $I_t$, equal to one on a success and zero on a failure. They satisfy $\mathbb E[I_t]=p$ and $\operatorname{Var}(I_t)=p(1-p)$. Since $K=I_1+\cdots+I_N$ for independent trials,
$$\mathbb E[K]=Np,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(K)=Np(1-p).$$
Dividing by $N$ gives the empirical proportion's mean and variance:
$$\mathbb E[\widehat p]=p,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p)=\frac{p(1-p)}{N}.$$
Thus a fourfold increase in sample size halves the standard deviation. A particular sample may still fluctuate more than an earlier smaller sample; the formula concerns the distribution over repeated samples.

## Rare categories and missing observations

If a category has probability $p>0$, the chance of missing it in all $N$ independent observations is $(1-p)^N$. A zero empirical count therefore does not establish that the underlying probability is zero. This matters when empirical probabilities will later appear inside logarithms or ratios.

::: {.example title="How often is a rare type missed?"}
A type with probability $1/20$ is absent from a sample of size $20$ with probability $(19/20)^{20}\approx0.3585$. Even though its expected count is one, observing no copies is quite common. At size $100$, the probability of missing it is approximately $0.00592$.
:::

The deterministic update of a composition and the random sampling of a composition can be combined in a two-stage model. First compute $p^+$ using a growth rule. Then draw a multinomial sample of size $N$ with probabilities $p^+$. The conditional average of the sampled composition is $p^+$, but the realized composition is a random lattice point. This provides a precise way to model finite-population variation.

## Exercises

::: {.exercises}
1. Draw the two-level probability tree for independent binary observations with $p=1/3$.
2. Find the probability of exactly two successes in four independent trials with success probability $1/3$.
3. List the six ordered binary samples of length four with two successes.
4. Derive the multinomial coefficient for three categories by choosing positions in two stages.
5. Find the multiplicity and probability of counts $(1,2,1)$ under $p=(1/2,1/3,1/6)$.
6. For readings $1,2,5$ with probabilities $1/4,1/2,1/4$, compute the mean and variance.
7. Prove the identity $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb E[X])^2$ by expansion.
8. For a binomial sample with $N=100$, $p=0.2$, find the mean and standard deviation of the count and of the empirical proportion.
9. A category has probability $0.1$. Find the first integer sample size for which its probability of being missed is below $0.05$. A calculator may be used.
10. Explain why observing zero copies of a category is compatible with a positive underlying probability. Give a numerical example.
11. For two independent samples of size $N$, find the variance of the difference of their empirical success proportions.
12. Describe a simulation of a population with normalized selection followed by multinomial sampling. Identify which stage is deterministic and which stage is random.
:::
