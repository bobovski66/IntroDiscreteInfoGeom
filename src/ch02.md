## A difference equation tells us how a state changes

A **difference equation** prescribes the next value of a sequence. For example,
$$\Delta x_n=-k(x_n-c)$$
says that each step corrects a fraction $k$ of the gap between the current state and a target $c$. Expanding the definition of $\Delta$ gives
$$x_{n+1}=c+(1-k)(x_n-c).$$
Repeated substitution yields the exact solution
$$\boxed{x_n=c+(1-k)^n(x_0-c).}$$
We call $c$ a **fixed point** because the update leaves it unchanged.

## How finite steps change stability

The size of $1-k$ determines whether the error shrinks. If $0<k<1$, every step approaches the target without crossing it. If $1<k<2$, successive corrections cross the target but get smaller. When $k=2$, the same-sized error alternates forever. If $k>2$, the error grows. The exact attracting condition is
$$\boxed{|1-k|<1,\quad\text{equivalently }0<k<2.}$$
For example, with $c=1/2$, $x_0=1/4$, and $k=3/2$, the first three states are $1/4,5/8,7/16$. The errors alternate signs and shrink by a factor of $1/2$ each step. These values stay within the probability interval for this initial state; a general affine feedback rule need not preserve that interval for every initial condition.

## Nonlinear difference equations on probabilities

More generally we write $x_{n+1}=F(x_n)$ and $\Delta x_n=F(x_n)-x_n$. A fixed point solves $F(c)=c$. To investigate local stability, start with nearby values and compare the distances $|x_n-c|$ after each step. If $F$ has an ordinary derivative available, the familiar local criterion is $|F'(c)|<1$; students can discover the same distinction numerically without using derivatives.

For a two-type population with scores $f_A(x),f_B(x)$, one possible positive update uses multipliers $w_i(x)=e^{\eta f_i(x)}$:
$$F(x)=\frac{xe^{\eta f_A(x)}}{xe^{\eta f_A(x)}+(1-x)e^{\eta f_B(x)}}.$$
Its finite difference $F(x)-x$ has the same sign as $f_A(x)-f_B(x)$ for $0<x<1$. Equal scores give a fixed point, but the size of the update still matters for stability.

## A calculation worth repeating

Using the payoff table in the following section, start on either side of an interior equal-score state. Compute $x_1$ and $\Delta x_0$, then repeat for a smaller and larger update intensity $\eta$. The comparison separates **direction** (the sign of change) from **step size** (how far the population actually moves). That distinction is central to discrete dynamics and control.

## Further investigations: Difference equations, feedback, and stability

An interaction changes the opportunities available to a type. A strategy may do well when it is rare and poorly when it becomes common. To describe this feedback, we assign scores to encounters and average them using the present composition. The resulting multipliers depend on the state, so the growth rule becomes a nonlinear difference equation.

## A matrix is a table of encounter scores

Consider two strategies, $A$ and $B$, with score table
$$M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.$$
The row specifies the focal strategy and the column specifies its opponent. Thus $b$ is the score of an $A$ individual meeting a $B$ individual. We assume opponents are encountered with frequencies $x$ and $1-x$. The average scores are
$$f_A(x)=ax+b(1-x),\qquad f_B(x)=cx+d(1-x).$$
The assumption about encounters is part of the model. Assortment by type, spatial clustering, or a rule excluding encounters with oneself can produce different averages.

The score difference is a straight-line expression:
$$\Delta(x)=f_A(x)-f_B(x)=(b-d)+(a-b-c+d)x.$$
Its sign records which strategy has the higher score at each composition. A zero of $\Delta$ is a candidate interior fixed point for the update rules below.

::: {.example title="A coexistence score table"}
For $M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&4\\3&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, the scores are $f_A=4-2x$ and $f_B=1+2x$. Their difference is $3-4x$, which vanishes at $q=3/4$. Below $q$, strategy $A$ has the higher score; above $q$, strategy $B$ has the higher score. At $q$, both scores are $5/2$.
:::

## Turning scores into positive multipliers

Scores and multipliers play different roles. A score may be negative, while a population multiplier must be positive. One possible conversion is the **linear multiplier rule**
$$w_i=1+\eta f_i,$$
where $\eta>0$ controls the strength of the update. This rule requires $1+\eta f_i>0$ for every score that can occur. If all encounter scores are at least $m<0$, choosing $\eta<-1/m$ is sufficient. Equality would permit zero multipliers and falls outside the positive rule used here.

Another conversion is the **exponential multiplier rule**
$$w_i=e^{\eta f_i}.$$
The number $e$ is approximately $2.71828$. The exponential is positive for every real input, and it satisfies $e^{u+v}=e^ue^v$. For computation, a calculator's exponential function is enough. Its algebraic properties are what we need in this course.

Normalizing linear multipliers gives
$$x^+=\frac{x(1+\eta f_A)}{1+\eta\bar f},\qquad
\bar f=xf_A+(1-x)f_B,$$
and consequently
$$x^+-x=\frac{\eta x(1-x)\Delta(x)}{1+\eta\bar f}.$$
Normalizing exponential multipliers gives
$$x^+=\frac{xe^{\eta f_A}}{xe^{\eta f_A}+(1-x)e^{\eta f_B}}.$$
For either conversion, the change has the sign of $\Delta$ in the interior. The amount of change depends on the conversion and on $\eta$.

::: {.example title="One score table, two finite rules"}
Use the coexistence table, $x=1/4$, and $\eta=1$. Then $(f_A,f_B)=(7/2,3/2)$. Linear multipliers are $(9/2,5/2)$, giving $x^+=3/8$. Exponential multipliers give $x^+=e^2/(e^2+3)\approx0.7112$. Both steps increase the $A$ proportion. Their sizes differ substantially.
:::

## Sign diagrams and fixed points

For positive multipliers, the pure states $0$ and $1$ are fixed. An interior state is fixed precisely when the two scores agree. When $a-b-c+d\ne0$, the candidate is
$$q=\frac{d-b}{a-b-c+d}.$$
It is an interior fixed point only if $0<q<1$. If the score difference is zero everywhere, every state is fixed. If the difference is a nonzero constant, there is no interior fixed point.

A **sign diagram** divides the interval at the zeros of $\Delta$ and records the direction of each single step. It provides useful information about motion. To prove convergence, we also need to control whether steps cross the proposed limit.

::: {.proposition title="Monotone coexistence for the linear rule"}
For the coexistence table and any $\eta>0$, the linear rule sends every interior initial state toward $q=3/4$ without crossing it, and the resulting sequence converges to $q$.
:::

To prove this, first compute $\bar f=1+5x-4x^2$. Since $\Delta=-4(x-q)$,
$$x^+-q=(x-q)\left(1-\frac{4\eta x(1-x)}{1+\eta\bar f}\right).$$
The factor in parentheses is strictly between zero and one for $0<x<1$. Positivity follows because subtracting $4\eta x(1-x)$ from the denominator leaves $1+\eta(1+x)>0$. The upper bound follows because the subtracted fraction is positive. Thus the error keeps its sign and decreases in magnitude.

A sequence starting below $q$ is increasing and bounded above by $q$; a sequence starting above $q$ is decreasing and bounded below by $q$. Such a sequence has a limit. Substituting its limit into this rational update is valid because the denominator stays positive. The limit must be a fixed point, and the interval between the initial state and $q$ contains no other fixed point. Therefore the limit is $q$.

::: {.example title="A coordination threshold"}
For $M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, we have $\Delta=4x-2$. If $x<1/2$, every step lowers $x$; if $x>1/2$, every step raises it. Both rules therefore move away from the threshold. The bounded sequences converge to $0$ and $1$, respectively. Starting exactly at $1/2$ leaves the state fixed.
:::

## What changes when a score table changes?

Exponential normalization is unchanged if a common number $C$ is added to all current scores: each weight gains the same factor $e^{\eta C}$, which cancels. Multiplying all scores by $c>0$ is equivalent to replacing $\eta$ by $c\eta$.

For linear multipliers, adding $C$ changes the effective intensity. When $1+\eta C>0$,
$$1+\eta(f_i+C)=(1+\eta C)\left(1+\frac{\eta}{1+\eta C}f_i\right).$$
The first factor cancels upon normalization, leaving intensity $\eta/(1+\eta C)$. Consequently, fixed points and signs may agree even though finite trajectories differ. This identity is useful when interpreting a table whose scores have been shifted to make them positive.

## Step size is part of the model

The exponential coexistence rule can cross its equilibrium. At $x=1/4$ and $\eta=2$, its next value is $e^4/(e^4+3)\approx0.9479$, beyond $3/4$. A sign diagram alone does not exclude this crossing. Repeated large steps can alternate across an equilibrium. Numerical experiments should report both the update rule and the chosen intensity, because they jointly determine the recurrence.
