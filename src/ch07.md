The probability triangle is an excellent map of compositions, but its ordinary Euclidean distances treat all coordinate changes in the same way. Statistical comparison also depends on where the probability mass sits. Taking square roots reveals another geometry, one in which a probability table becomes a point on a sphere.

This chapter uses lengths, dot products, and angles. Angles will be measured in radians: a full turn is $2\pi$, a right angle is $\pi/2$, and an arc of angle $\alpha$ on a circle of radius $R$ has length $R\alpha$.

## Square roots place probabilities on a sphere

Map a probability vector $p$ to the vector
$$s(p)=(2\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,2\sqrt{p_k}).$$
Its squared length is $\sum_i(2\sqrt{p_i})^2=4\sum_i p_i=4$. Thus $s(p)$ lies on a sphere of radius $2$. Every coordinate is nonnegative, so only the nonnegative portion of the sphere is used. Conversely, a nonnegative vector $s$ of length $2$ gives probabilities $p_i=s_i^2/4$.

For two categories, the image is a quarter circle. For three, it is an octant of an ordinary sphere. The same formula works for more categories, although a direct spatial picture is then unavailable. Dot products and lengths still make sense as finite sums.

::: {.example title="Pure and uniform distributions"}
The pure three-category state $(1,0,0)$ maps to $(2,0,0)$. The uniform state maps to $(2/\sqrt3,2/\sqrt3,2/\sqrt3)$. Two different pure states lie at right angles as viewed from the center of the sphere. They are maximally separated within its nonnegative portion.
:::

## Overlap determines the angle

The **Bhattacharyya coefficient**, or square-root overlap, is
$$B(p,r)=\sum_i\sqrt{p_ir_i}.$$
The dot product of the two sphere vectors is $s(p)\cdot s(r)=4B(p,r)$. Since both lengths are $2$, the cosine of their angle $\alpha$ is $B(p,r)$. The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality ensures $B\leq1$:
$$\left(\sum_i\sqrt{p_ir_i}\right)^2\leq\left(\sum_ip_i\right)\left(\sum_ir_i\right)=1.$$
Also $B\geq0$. The angle therefore lies in $[0,\pi/2]$.

We define the **categorical Fisher--Rao distance** by the length of the shorter spherical arc:
$$d(p,r)=2\arccos B(p,r).$$
This normalization uses the sphere of radius $2$. Equality of the tables gives distance zero; disjoint supports give distance $\pi$. The construction is a standard finite probability geometry. Its arc formula can be used without developing differential geometry.

::: {.example title="A distance calculated exactly"}
Let $p=(1,0)$ and $r=(1/4,3/4)$. Their overlap is $1/2$, so their angle is $\pi/3$ and their distance is $2\pi/3$. The coordinate difference of the first probabilities is $3/4$; it measures a different feature from the spherical distance.
:::

## Chords, arcs, and Hellinger distance

The straight chord joining the sphere vectors has squared length
$$\|s(p)-s(r)\|^2=8\bigl(1-B(p,r)\bigr).$$
To verify this, expand the square and use the squared lengths $4$ and the dot product $4B$. The **Hellinger distance** with the convention used here is
$$h(p,r)=\sqrt{1-B(p,r)}
=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(\sum_i(\sqrt{p_i}-\sqrt{r_i})^2\right)^{1/2}.$$
Thus the chord length is $2\sqrt2\,h$. The arc and chord are different, but both increase as overlap decreases. Hellinger distance, spherical distance, and relative entropy should always be identified by their formulas, since their numerical scales and boundary behavior differ.

::: {.example title="Behavior at zero probabilities"}
For $p=(1,0)$ and $r=(0,1)$, the overlap is zero, the Hellinger distance is one, and the spherical distance is $\pi$. Both directed relative entropies are infinite. The square-root construction includes the boundary of the probability simplex at finite distance.
:::

## A complete coordinate for binary probabilities

For a binary distribution $(x,1-x)$, put
$$\phi=\arcsin\sqrt{x},\qquad 0\leq\phi\leq\pi/2.$$
Then $x=\sin^2\phi$ and $s=(2\sin\phi,2\cos\phi)$. The angle $\phi$ specifies a point on the quarter circle. Since the radius is $2$, the arc coordinate measured from $x=0$ is $2\phi$. Two binary distributions therefore have distance
$$d\bigl((x,1-x),(y,1-y)\bigr)=2\left|\arcsin\sqrt{x}-\arcsin\sqrt{y}\right|.$$
This gives a useful distinction between equal changes in probability and equal changes in statistical distance.

::: {.example title="Equal probability increments at different locations"}
The distance from $x=0$ to $x=1/4$ is $\pi/3$. The distance from $x=1/4$ to $x=1/2$ is $\pi/6$. Both changes add $1/4$ to the probability, yet the first covers twice the spherical arc. Near a boundary, introducing probability into a previously absent category can be a substantial geometric change.
:::

## Why the arc formula is a distance

The shorter great-circle arc joins any two points on a sphere unless they are antipodal. Our nonnegative points are never antipodal. Elementary spherical geometry identifies this arc as a shortest spherical route. Concatenating a route from $p$ to $q$ with one from $q$ to $r$ gives a route from $p$ to $r$, whose length cannot be smaller than the shortest route. Hence
$$d(p,r)\leq d(p,q)+d(q,r).$$
This is the triangle inequality. Symmetry follows from symmetry of overlap, and zero distance forces equal square-root coordinates, hence equal probabilities. These properties make $d$ a distance. In Chapter 8 we will construct intermediate points on the shortest arc using only trigonometry.

When calculations are performed on a computer, tiny rounding errors can push an overlap slightly above one. Before applying inverse cosine, clamp the computed overlap to $[0,1]$. Exact mathematics already guarantees this interval; the adjustment handles floating-point arithmetic rather than changing the definition.

## Exercises

::: {.exercises}
1. Map $(1/4,1/4,1/2)$ to the radius-two sphere and verify its squared length.
2. Recover the distribution represented by $s=(1,1,\sqrt2)$.
3. Compute the overlap between $(1/2,1/2,0)$ and $(1/2,0,1/2)$.
4. Find the spherical distance and Hellinger distance for the pair in Exercise 3.
5. Prove the chord-length formula by expanding a squared norm.
6. Show that $B(p,r)=0$ exactly when $p$ and $r$ have disjoint supports.
7. Show that $B(p,r)=1$ exactly when $p=r$, using the equality condition in Cauchy--Schwarz.
8. Find the binary arc coordinates of $x=0,1/4,1/2,3/4,1$.
9. Compare the distances from $0.01$ to $0.02$ and from $0.49$ to $0.50$ for binary probabilities.
10. Give two distributions whose relative entropy is infinite but whose spherical distance is strictly smaller than $\pi$.
11. Explain the difference between the angle $\alpha$, the radius-two arc length $2\alpha$, and the chord length $4\sin(\alpha/2)$.
12. Write an algorithm that computes overlap and spherical distance for any two finite probability tables. Include validation of the inputs and handling of rounding near one.
:::

## Selected solutions

**1.** The image is $(1,1,\sqrt2)$, whose squared length is $1+1+2=4$.

**3.** Only the first label contributes, so $B=1/2$.

**4.** The spherical distance is $2\pi/3$ and the Hellinger distance is $1/\sqrt2$.

**6.** Each summand is nonnegative. Their sum is zero exactly when every product $p_ir_i$ is zero, meaning no label has positive probability in both tables.

**8.** The arc coordinates are $0,\pi/3,\pi/2,2\pi/3,\pi$.

**10.** For $p=(1,0)$ and $r=(1/2,1/2)$, $D(r\|p)=\infty$, while their spherical distance is $\pi/2$.
