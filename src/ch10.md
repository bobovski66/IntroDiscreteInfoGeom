## Information moves on a network

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a finite undirected graph. A probability distribution places mass $p_i(n)\geq0$ at each vertex, with total mass one. Give each undirected edge a single orientation for bookkeeping and denote the signed current from $i$ to $j$ by $J_{ij}(n)=-J_{ji}(n)$.

The **discrete divergence** at a vertex is net outward current,
$$\operatorname{div}J(i)=\sum_{j\sim i}J_{ij}.$$
Then the conservation law is the discrete continuity equation
$$\boxed{p_i(n+1)-p_i(n)=-\operatorname{div}J_n(i).}$$
Add this equation over every vertex. Each edge current cancels with its opposite, so the total probability remains one. This is a finite analogue of the divergence theorem.

## A graph gradient and Laplacian

For a scalar field $f$ on the vertices, the gradient along an oriented edge $i\to j$ is $f_j-f_i$. A diffusion current flowing from higher to lower probability can be chosen as
$$J_{ij}=\tau(p_i-p_j),\qquad\tau>0.$$
Substitution yields
$$\boxed{p_i(n+1)=p_i(n)+\tau\sum_{j\sim i}(p_j(n)-p_i(n)).}$$
The sum is a **graph Laplacian** applied to $p$. It measures how a vertex differs from its neighbors.

To ensure nonnegative probabilities at every step, choose $\tau\leq1/d_{\max}$, where $d_{\max}$ is the largest vertex degree. Then the update matrix is stochastic. Because the graph is undirected, its rows also sum to one, so it is doubly stochastic.

## Three vertices, one exact step

On the path $A$–$B$–$C$, choose $\tau=1/4$ and start at $p_0=(1,0,0)$. The step gives
$$p_1=(3/4,1/4,0).$$
The next gives
$$p_2=(5/8,5/16,1/16).$$
Compute $\Delta p_0$ and $\Delta p_1$. In each case the three coordinates sum to zero, because the changes are redistribution. Repeating the update brings the distribution toward the stationary uniform state.

## Entropy and the direction of change

Because this symmetric diffusion is doubly stochastic, it does not decrease Shannon entropy:
$$H(p_{n+1})-H(p_n)\geq0.$$
One way to prove this is to apply the contraction of relative entropy toward the uniform distribution $u$. Since $D(p\|u)=\log|V|-H(p)$ and $Ku=u$,
$$D(Kp\|u)\leq D(p\|u).$$
Hence $\Delta H_n\geq0$. The conclusion depends on the **doubly stochastic** assumption. An arbitrary stochastic channel may lower entropy.

## The course's complete mathematical circle

We started with $\Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n$. Now the same operation measures probability changes, Lyapunov dissipation, and entropy production, while the graph gradient and divergence describe where probability travels. The fundamental theorem for finite differences and the cancellation of internal fluxes explain why discrete conservation laws work.

Use finite calculations to explain what the mathematics says about information moving across the network.

## Further investigations: Graph divergence, diffusion, and information flow

An observation can be recorded at several resolutions. A detailed label may be replaced by a broader group, passed through a noisy channel, or transported among nodes of a network. These operations transform probability tables by finite weighted sums. They connect graph dynamics with a basic principle of information geometry: applying the same observation rule to two models cannot make them more distinguishable by the discrepancies studied here.

## A stochastic matrix describes one step

Let $K_{ji}$ be the probability of arriving at output label $j$ from input label $i$. Our convention places the input in the column. Thus
$$K_{ji}\geq0,\qquad \sum_jK_{ji}=1\quad\hbox{for each }i.$$
Such a table is **column-stochastic**. If input probabilities are $p_i$, the output probabilities are
$$p_j^+=\sum_iK_{ji}p_i,\qquad p^+=Kp.$$
The product symbol $Kp$ abbreviates these weighted sums. Adding over $j$ gives $\sum_jp_j^+=\sum_ip_i\sum_jK_{ji}=1$. This is a linear difference equation on probability vectors.

Repeated application gives $p_t=K^tp_0$. A probability vector $\pi$ is **stationary** if $K\pi=\pi$. Stationarity is a fixed-point condition. It does not by itself imply that every trajectory converges to $\pi$.

::: {.example title="A lazy walk on a three-node path"}
Consider
$$K=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&1/4&0\\1/4&1/2&1/4\\0&1/4&3/4\end{pmatrix}.$$
Each column describes remaining at a node or moving to a neighbor. Starting at $p_0=(1,0,0)$ gives $p_1=(3/4,1/4,0)$ and $p_2=(5/8,5/16,1/16)$. The uniform table is stationary because the rows, as well as the columns, sum to one. This particular walk approaches uniform, as can be checked from its repeated finite updates.
:::

A matrix whose rows and columns both sum to one is **doubly stochastic**. The cyclic permutation matrix is also doubly stochastic, but every noncentral three-category state repeats with period three. To establish convergence in a general network, additional conditions are required. Our contraction inequalities below concern one-step comparisons and do not require a claim of convergence.

## Coarse-graining is a deterministic channel

Partition the input labels into groups. The output reports only the group containing the input. Set $K_{ji}=1$ if label $i$ belongs to group $j$, and zero otherwise. Each column contains exactly one entry equal to one. The output probabilities are sums over the groups.

::: {.example title="A distinction erased by grouping"}
Let $q=(0.4,0.1,0.4,0.1)$ and $p=(0.1,0.4,0.1,0.4)$. Group labels $1,2$ together and labels $3,4$ together. Both output distributions are $(1/2,1/2)$. Before grouping, $D(q\|p)=0.6\ln4\approx0.8318$. After grouping, it is zero. The within-group differences cannot be recovered from the recorded group alone.
:::

## The log-sum inequality

Let $a_i,b_i$ be nonnegative numbers with positive totals $A=\sum_i a_i$ and $B=\sum_i b_i$, and suppose $b_i>0$ wherever $a_i>0$. Applying Gibbs' inequality to $a_i/A$ and $b_i/B$ gives
$$\sum_i a_i\ln\frac{a_i}{b_i}\geq A\ln\frac AB.$$
To see this directly, multiply $D(a/A\|b/B)\geq0$ by $A$ and expand the logarithm. This is the **log-sum inequality**. It compares the divergence contributions before and after a set of entries is merged.

Apply it separately to each output label $j$, using $a_i=K_{ji}q_i$ and $b_i=K_{ji}p_i$. The output totals are $(Kq)_j$ and $(Kp)_j$. Summing over outputs gives
$$D(Kq\|Kp)\leq\sum_{j,i}K_{ji}q_i\ln\frac{q_i}{p_i}
=D(q\|p),$$
because each column of $K$ sums to one. Terms with $K_{ji}=0$ contribute zero. The usual support conventions cover zero totals and infinite divergences.

::: {.proposition title="Data processing for relative entropy"}
For a column-stochastic matrix applied to both distributions, $D(Kq\|Kp)\leq D(q\|p)$.
:::

The same channel must be used for both tables. Applying different recording rules can create differences caused by those rules themselves. For deterministic grouping with positive probabilities, equality holds exactly when $q_i/p_i$ is constant within each group: no distinction relevant to that ratio is lost within a group.

## Square-root overlap also contracts distance

For each output label, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$$\sqrt{\left(\sum_iK_{ji}q_i\right)\left(\sum_iK_{ji}p_i\right)}
\geq\sum_iK_{ji}\sqrt{q_ip_i}.$$
Adding over $j$ and using column sums yields
$$B(Kq,Kp)\geq B(q,p).$$
Since inverse cosine decreases as its argument increases, the spherical distance satisfies
$$d(Kq,Kp)\leq d(q,p).$$
Hellinger distance also cannot increase, because its square is $1-B$. Thus two different constructions, a logarithmic discrepancy and a square-root distance, both obey an information-loss principle under the same finite observation rule.

::: {.example title="A noisy binary observation"}
Suppose a binary label is flipped with probability $1/4$, so $K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3/4&1/4\\1/4&3/4\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. The tables $(0.9,0.1)$ and $(0.1,0.9)$ become $(0.7,0.3)$ and $(0.3,0.7)$. Their overlap increases from $0.6$ to $2\sqrt{0.21}\approx0.9165$. Their spherical distance decreases from approximately $1.8546$ to $0.8230$.
:::

## When does entropy increase on a network?

A stochastic channel can lower entropy. A channel that sends every input to one output converts every distribution into a certain outcome with entropy zero. The data-processing inequality compares two models through the same channel; entropy measures the uncertainty of one table. The questions are related but distinct.

For a doubly stochastic matrix, uniform $u$ is stationary. Data processing then gives
$$D(Kp\|u)=D(Kp\|Ku)\leq D(p\|u).$$
Using $D(p\|u)=\ln k-H(p)$ shows $H(Kp)\geq H(p)$. This is an exact entropy-increase statement with a specific assumption on the channel. Equality can occur at a nonuniform table, as a permutation simply rearranges its probabilities.

## A finite statistical family

A collection of probability tables indexed by one or more parameters is a **statistical family**. On fixed labels $x_i$, for example, the weights
$$p_i(m,a)=\frac{e^{-a(x_i-m)^2}}{\sum_je^{-a(x_j-m)^2}},\qquad a>0,$$
form a finite bell-shaped family. Changing $m$ shifts the emphasis across the labels, and changing $a$ changes its concentration. These are normalized weights on a fixed finite set. Their entropy, relative entropy, and spherical distance can all be computed by the formulas already developed. Grouping labels produces a coarser family and contracts pairwise discrepancies by the same theorems.

This offers a capstone investigation: choose two parameter values, calculate their tables, apply a recording channel, and quantify the distinctions retained. The finite sums, token graphs, and exact updates developed throughout the course provide the tools for carrying out the investigation and explaining its assumptions.
