## Relative entropy compares distributions

For positive probability tables $p$ and $q$, define the relative entropy
$$D(p\|q)=\sum_i p_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_i}.$$
It is generally asymmetric: changing the order of $p$ and $q$ changes the number. Nevertheless, it is always nonnegative and equals zero exactly when the two tables agree. We can use it as a potential measuring the discrepancy from a reference state.

Suppose $p_n$ changes in discrete time while $q$ remains fixed. Define
$$V_n=D(p_n\|q),\qquad \Delta V_n=V_{n+1}-V_n.$$
This is an ordinary first difference of a sequence, even though each term comes from a function on a probability simplex. If $\Delta V_n\leq0$, we call $V$ a **discrete Lyapunov function** for the chosen update: its value never increases along an orbit.

## A mixing rule with a known fixed point

Fix a positive reference distribution $q$ and a mixing strength $0\leq\lambda\leq1$. Define
$$p_{n+1}=(1-\lambda)p_n+\lambda q.$$
The error $p_n-q$ is multiplied by $1-\lambda$ every step:
$$p_n-q=(1-\lambda)^n(p_0-q).$$
For $0<\lambda\leq1$, the distribution approaches $q$. This is the vector form of the stable scalar difference equation from Week 2.

The convexity of relative entropy in its first argument gives
$$D((1-\lambda)p+\lambda q\|q)\leq(1-\lambda)D(p\|q).$$
Consequently
$$\boxed{\Delta V_n\leq-\lambda V_n.}$$
This is a quantitative stability statement: a whole step reduces KL discrepancy by at least the stated fraction. The derivation uses a finite inequality, not an infinitesimal derivative.

## A concrete calculation

Take $p_0=(3/4,1/4)$, $q=(1/2,1/2)$ and $\lambda=1/2$. Then
$$p_1=(5/8,3/8),\qquad p_2=(9/16,7/16).$$
Calculate $D(p_0\|q)$, $D(p_1\|q)$, and $D(p_2\|q)$ on a calculator. Each is positive and smaller than its predecessor. Plotting those numbers against $n$ displays a *Lyapunov sequence*.

## Discrete integration of dissipation

Apply the discrete fundamental theorem to the Lyapunov sequence:
$$\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}\big(V_n-V_{n+1}\big)=V_0-V_T.$$
The left side is the total discrepancy dissipated across $T$ steps. If $V_n$ converges to zero, the infinite sum of the one-step losses equals $V_0$.

The sections that follow develop Gibbs' inequality and finite contraction results. We can now interpret these inequalities dynamically, as bounds on the discrete derivative of an information potential.

## Further investigations: Relative entropy and discrete Lyapunov theory

Two probability tables can describe competing models of the same observations. Relative entropy measures a discrepancy between those tables, with a definite role for each argument. Its most useful properties follow from finite inequalities. We will also construct a recurrence for which relative entropy decreases at a provable rate.

## Cross-entropy and relative entropy

Suppose observations follow distribution $q$, while a proposed model assigns probabilities $p$. The average surprisal under the proposed model is the **cross-entropy**
$$H(q,p)=-\sum_iq_i\ln p_i.$$
Subtracting the actual entropy of $q$ defines the **Kullback--Leibler divergence**, or **relative entropy**:
$$D(q\|p)=\sum_iq_i\ln\frac{q_i}{p_i}=H(q,p)-H(q).$$
All logarithms in this chapter are natural, so the units are nats. To express the same quantity in bits, divide by $\ln2$. Terms with $q_i=0$ contribute zero. If $q_i>0$ and $p_i=0$ for any label, the divergence is infinite: the proposed model makes an actually possible observation impossible.

::: {.example title="The argument order matters"}
For $q=(1/2,1/2)$ and $p=(3/4,1/4)$,
$$D(q\|p)=\tfrac12\ln(2/3)+\tfrac12\ln2=\tfrac12\ln(4/3)\approx0.1438.$$
Reversing the arguments gives $(3/4)\ln(3/2)+(1/4)\ln(1/2)\approx0.1308$. The values differ. Relative entropy is a directed discrepancy rather than a distance satisfying symmetry.
:::

## Nonnegativity from an arithmetic-geometric inequality

First assume every $q_i$ is positive and all corresponding $p_i$ are positive. Weighted arithmetic-geometric mean gives
$$\prod_i\left(\frac{p_i}{q_i}\right)^{q_i}\leq\sum_iq_i\frac{p_i}{q_i}=1.$$
Taking logarithms and reversing the sign proves $D(q\|p)\geq0$. If some $q_i$ vanish, apply the inequality on the support of $q$; the sum on the right is then at most one. The same conclusion holds. Equality requires $p=q$, including agreement on the zero entries.

This is **Gibbs' inequality**. It implies that, averaged under $q$, no proposed probability table has a smaller logarithmic surprise than $q$ itself. The conclusion is about a specified average; it does not say that every single observation favors the same model.

::: {.example title="Uniform reference and entropy"}
Let $u_i=1/k$. Expanding the definition gives
$$D(p\|u)=\sum_i p_i\ln p_i+\ln k=\ln k-H(p).$$
Gibbs' inequality therefore gives $H(p)\leq\ln k$, and equality occurs exactly at the uniform distribution. Relative entropy expresses the gap from maximum entropy.
:::

## Finite data give an exact likelihood comparison

Suppose an ordered sample has counts $n_i$ and empirical distribution $\widehat q_i=n_i/N$. Under independent observations, its likelihood under model $p$ is $L(p)=\prod_i p_i^{n_i}$. Taking logarithms,
$$\ln L(p)=N\sum_i\widehat q_i\ln p_i.$$
Consequently,
$$\ln L(\widehat q)-\ln L(p)=N D(\widehat q\|p).$$
The same identity holds for the likelihood of the count vector, since its multinomial coefficient cancels in the ratio. It proves that the empirical table maximizes the likelihood over all distributions, using only Gibbs' inequality. A table with zero entries can maximize likelihood for the observed sample while assigning zero probability to a category that could appear in a later sample.

::: {.example title="Comparing a sample with a proposed model"}
For ten binary observations with counts $(8,2)$, compare the empirical table $(0.8,0.2)$ with the fair model $(0.5,0.5)$. The divergence is $0.8\ln1.6+0.2\ln0.4\approx0.1927$. The log-likelihood advantage is ten times this, approximately $1.9274$, and the likelihood ratio is approximately $6.872$.
:::

## A recurrence with guaranteed contraction

Fix a target distribution $q$ and choose $0<\lambda\leq1$. Define the **mixture update**
$$p^+=(1-\lambda)p+\lambda q.$$
Each coordinate is an ordinary weighted average. The difference from the target satisfies $p^+-q=(1-\lambda)(p-q)$. Repeating this identity yields
$$p_t=(1-\lambda)^t p_0+\bigl[1-(1-\lambda)^t\bigr]q.$$
Thus the target is approached geometrically when $0<\lambda<1$ and is reached in one step when $\lambda=1$.

Relative entropy satisfies a matching one-step bound:
$$D(q\|p^+)\leq(1-\lambda)D(q\|p).$$
For $0<\lambda<1$ and positive entries, arithmetic-geometric mean gives, coordinate by coordinate,
$$p_i^+=(1-\lambda)p_i+\lambda q_i\geq p_i^{1-\lambda}q_i^\lambda.$$
Taking logarithms, multiplying by $q_i$, and adding proves the bound. Zero terms can be handled by the stated conventions, or by approaching them with positive tables. The case $\lambda=1$ is immediate. Iteration gives $D(q\|p_t)\leq(1-\lambda)^tD(q\|p_0)$ whenever the initial divergence is finite.

::: {.example title="Checking the bound numerically"}
Take $q=(1/2,1/2)$, $p=(0.9,0.1)$, and $\lambda=1/2$. Then $p^+=(0.7,0.3)$. The old divergence is approximately $0.5108$ and the new divergence approximately $0.08718$. The bound permits at most $0.2554$, so it holds with room to spare. A bound need not be an equality to be useful.
:::

The recurrence was chosen to have this property. A state-dependent payoff update is a different rule and requires its own analysis. A plotted decrease over a short run provides evidence for that run; an algebraic bound establishes the claim for every state covered by its assumptions.

## Mixing and the value of entropy

Let $m=(1-\lambda)p+\lambda r$. Expanding the definitions gives the identity
$$H(m)-(1-\lambda)H(p)-\lambda H(r)
=(1-\lambda)D(p\|m)+\lambda D(r\|m).$$
The right side is nonnegative, so entropy is **concave**: the entropy of a mixture is at least the mixture of the entropies. For $0<\lambda<1$, equality holds precisely when $p=r$. The identity explains how much uncertainty is gained when two different probability tables are pooled without retaining their source label.

As another application of Gibbs' inequality, let a prefix code have lengths $\ell_i$ and Kraft sum $C=\sum_i2^{-\ell_i}\leq1$. Put $r_i=2^{-\ell_i}/C$. Then
$$D(p\|r)=-H(p)+(\ln2)\sum_i p_i\ell_i+\ln C\geq0.$$
Hence the expected code length satisfies $\sum_i p_i\ell_i\geq H_2(p)-\log_2C\geq H_2(p)$. The lower bound comes from a probability inequality combined with a counting restriction on trees.

8. Starting with finite $D(q\|p_0)$, find a sufficient integer number of mixture steps to reduce its bound by a factor of $16$ when $\lambda=1/2$.
9. Derive the entropy concavity identity by expanding all four terms.
10. Take $p=(1,0)$ and $r=(0,1)$. Compare their entropies with the entropy of their equally weighted mixture.
11. Use the prefix-code argument to bound the expected length of a code with lengths $(2,2,2)$ under uniform probabilities on three labels.

## Selected solutions

**1.** The divergence is $\ln4$. If $p_1=0$, it is infinite.

**5.** The direct likelihood ratio is $[(3/4)^3(1/4)]/(1/2)^4=27/16$. Taking logarithms also gives
$$4D\bigl((3/4,1/4)\|(1/2,1/2)\bigr)=\ln(27/16).$$

**6.** The first two states are $(5/6,1/12,1/12)$ and $(17/24,7/48,7/48)$.

**8.** Four steps suffice for the bound, since $(1/2)^4=1/16$. The actual divergence may decrease faster.

**10.** The two unmixed entropies are zero. The equal mixture has entropy $\ln2$ nats.

**11.** The Kraft sum is $3/4$. The stronger bound is $\log_23-\log_2(3/4)=2$ bits. The actual expected length is exactly two bits.
