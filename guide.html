Course guide

Teaching and studying the course

The course develops information geometry from exact finite operations. Its sequence moves from counts and proportions to statistical discrepancy, spherical paths, and information networks.

Prerequisites and pacing

Students should be comfortable with fractions, powers, linear equations, and plotting points. Probability, matrix products, logarithms, factorials, and the necessary trigonometry are introduced in the readings. Each week can be organized as two meetings: one for concepts and worked calculations, another for a guided investigation and discussion. The laboratory can also be completed independently.

Assessment

Each chapter has twelve exercises and six selected solutions. Each weekly lab has five investigations; the final investigation in Week 10 is a capstone report. Each quiz has eight questions with explanatory feedback. A possible assessment balance is 40% exercises, 30% laboratories, 10% quizzes, and 20% capstone. Instructors can adapt the balance and choose which exercises to collect.

Mathematical choices

The starting recurrence normalizes positive count multipliers. Linear multipliers require a positivity check; exponential multipliers are positive by construction. Finite cyclic selection can change a coordinate product that is conserved in a continuous-time treatment. The course proves the finite product identity directly. Its geometry uses the exact square-root sphere and its arc distance; it does not require an infinitesimal metric construction.

The learning chapter solves a KL-penalized problem through an algebraic identity. The network chapter proves data processing by log-sum and Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities. These results provide a coherent endpoint with finite models and finite proofs.

Capstone report

Choose two positive probability models on four to eight labels. Explain the labels and give the two tables. Specify a channel that groups, perturbs, or transports the labels. Compute both directed relative entropies, square-root overlap, and spherical distance before and after the channel. Include an exact calculation for at least one quantity, a reproducible table of numerical values, and a figure that helps explain the result. Interpret what the recording rule retains and what it loses. State all assumptions and distinguish numerical observations from claims established by a proof.

Using the materials

The site is a collection of static pages. Demonstrations and quiz feedback run in the browser. Lab responses and completion marks use local browser storage when available; they are not submitted to an instructor. Download lab answers to keep a separate copy. Clearing browser data can remove saved work. Mathematical typesetting uses an online MathJax script; the compiled PDFs are suitable for offline reading.

Further reading

For multiplicative weights, see Arora, Hazan, and Kale, The multiplicative weights update method (2012). For finite Markov chains, see Levin and Peres, Markov Chains and Mixing Times, second edition (2017). Nielsen’s An elementary introduction to information geometry (2020) develops the wider geometric setting.
