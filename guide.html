Course guide

Teaching and studying discrete information geometry

This course builds a calculus from exact finite steps. We begin with differences of sequences, then work on probability lattices, and finally calculate how information moves through networks.

The central mathematical idea

A discrete derivative subtracts neighboring values. A discrete integral adds changes. On the probability simplex, neighboring states differ by a one-token transfer, so entropy and other information quantities have exact directional differences. Assigning currents to oriented graph edges leads to divergence, a continuity equation, and conservation of probability. Statistical distance provides the geometry in which those movements are measured.

Preparation

Students should know fractions, powers, basic algebra, and coordinate graphs. Logarithms, elementary probability, matrices, and the needed trigonometry are introduced when used. No continuous calculus is assumed.

Weekly structure

Read the new calculus section first and work through its numerical examples. Explore the original topic demonstration and the discrete calculus operator explorer. Complete the five-part guided investigation, then use the eight-question check-in for feedback. The original counting and finite probability examples are retained as applications of the new operations.

Mathematical conventions

The forward difference is Δaₙ = aₙ₊₁ − aₙ, and summing differences telescopes to the endpoint difference. Probability vectors are nonnegative and sum to one. Token-transfer directions are legal only when the donor category is occupied. Edge currents are antisymmetric, and positive divergence means net outward flow. The continuity equation is Δp = −div J. The Fisher quadratic length is a local approximation at positive distributions; exact square-root spherical distance is defined even at the boundary.

Final investigation

Choose a graph with four or five vertices, a starting probability distribution, and a symmetric diffusion step small enough to preserve nonnegative values. Calculate at least four iterates. Record current, divergence, the probability difference vector, and Shannon entropy at each step. Verify conservation directly by summing your differences. Calculate a Fisher–Rao distance between two time steps and explain how the information distribution changes.

Further study

For the continuous development of simplex and Fisher geometry, use the companion course.
